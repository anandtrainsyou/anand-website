You can still get stronger in a calorie deficit. Losing strength on a diet is common, but it isn't automatic. It usually comes down to three things:

Inadequate programming
Improper nutrition
Sleep that's all over the place

Get those three dialed in and you can keep getting stronger, even in a calorie deficit.

FAQ

Putting it together

What is progressive overload?

A rise in performance while everything else stays the same, like your form and the setup. For most people that's one more rep or a bit more weight: you're getting stronger. Over months, this is what builds the armor.

For advanced lifters it can show up in slightly different forms, but we'll save that for another day.

What it isn't
  • Adding weight every session, no matter what.
  • Going to failure on every set. It piles on fatigue and costs you the next session.
  • Letting your form slip to move more weight.
  • Switching programs every week to chase that early jump. It's mostly your nervous system learning the lift, not new muscle.
How do I progress?

Reps first, then weight. Each set has a rep target. Once you beat it at the same RIR, add load.

Example: your first set is 8 reps and you get 9. Raise the weight for the second set. You'll probably land nearer 6, and that's the plan working. Keep that weight until you beat it again.

How hard should a set be?

0 to 2 reps in reserve. Past that, most people are bad at guessing how many reps they had left.

Going to failure is also a skill. You learn it by practising it on purpose, not on every set.

How long do I rest?
  • At least 2 minutes, even on single-arm and single-leg lifts, because the fatigue carries over.
  • Very taxing compounds: 3 to 4 minutes, up to 5 is fine.
Warm-up
  1. 5 to 8 minutes of cardio to warm up your joints and body
  2. 50% of your working weight × 5 to 6 reps
  3. 70 to 75% × 3 reps
  4. 80 to 90% × 1 to 2 reps
  5. Rest 30 seconds, then start your working set
Form cues for every lift
  • Same depth every rep. Use the range of motion that works the muscle, without straining your joints.
  • Full range, under control. Lower the weight slowly (the eccentric). Lift it with control, not an explosion (the concentric).
  • Stay in control for the whole set. Losing coordination is how joint positions change.
  • Failure is the last rep you can do with the same form. If you're getting partials, you've changed the exercise: that's not failure.
Why log your workouts?

Without a log you can't tell if your performance is going up. Compare each session to the last one, then compare month to month.

You don't need to progress every session. Beginners can. Intermediates can every other session. If you're more trained, spend 1 to 2 sessions getting used to a weight and its form, then let the progress show up.
